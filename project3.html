A Small Diffusion Language Model for Children's Stories

Writing a whole story at once, then fixing it in a few steps

Project 3 tutorial

Projects 1 and 2 built models that write left to right, one token at a time. Here we build a different kind of model, a masked diffusion model. It starts from a page of blanks and fills them in over a few rounds, many blanks at once. Then we test whether it can match the left-to-right models of the same size and the same training data, and whether it is faster. Everything runs on a normal computer's CPU.

This page explains every step: what we did, why, what result we got, and how that result told us what to do next. You don't need to read Projects 1–2 first. Idea 1 recaps them.

The one question

Can a small diffusion language model write stories as well as a left-to-right model of the same size, and is it faster?

The one idea behind everything

Left to right (Projects 1–2):   Tom → had → a → big → red → ball → .        one token per model call
Diffusion (Project 3):          _ _ _ _ _ _ _  →  _ had _ big _ ball _  →  Tom had a big red ball .
                                every blank is guessed in every round; a few rounds in total

The short answer

No, not at this size. The diffusion model writes clearly worse stories. It is much faster only when it uses very few rounds, and then its stories fall apart.

The pipeline (the order we did things in)

Same tools & data→ Add a [MASK] token→ Hide tokens→ Model that looks both ways→ Train 2 models→ Fill-in test→ Write by unmasking→ Tune on practice prompts→ Compare with AR→ Read the stories→ Final answer

Idea 1 What Projects 1 and 2 built

Two left-to-right story writers that we now use as the yardstick.

A language model gives a probability to every possible next token (a word or a piece of a word; our tokenizer knows 8,192 of them). To write, it picks a token, adds it, and guesses again. This is called autoregressive (AR) writing.

  • Project 1 trained a 13.9-million-parameter AR model on 1.77 million children's stories (TinyStories). Test perplexity 5.74. We call it the AR teacher.
  • Project 2 trained a smaller 5.3M AR model, alone and by copying the teacher (distillation). The one trained alone (test perplexity 9.63) is our AR small model.

Project 3 trains two diffusion models with exactly these two sizes and the same data, so the only difference is the way of writing.

two runners of the same height and the same training hours, but one runs and the other cycles. If one wins, it's the method, not the body.

Idea 2 Two ways to write a story

One token per model call, or every blank at once, a few times.

An AR model needs one model call per token: a 200-token story is 200 calls, one after another.

A diffusion model starts from a window of 256 blanks. In each step (round) it guesses every blank at once, keeps some of its guesses and leaves the rest blank. After S steps (we tried 4 to 32) there are no blanks left. So it needs S model calls, whatever the story length. That is the promise: speed.

AR writing is a person typing a letter word by word. Diffusion is a person sketching the whole page roughly, then going over it a few times, filling more gaps each time.

Idea 3 The [MASK] token and hiding tokens

Training is a fill-in-the-blanks game with a random amount of blanks.

We add one new token to the vocabulary, [MASK] (number 8192), meaning "blank". To make a training example we take a real story, pick a masking level t between 0 and 1 at random, and hide each token with probability t. Sometimes 5% of the tokens are hidden, sometimes 95%. The model must guess the hidden ones.

  • The story's first token, <BOS> (begin of story), is never hidden: it tells the model "a story starts here".
  • The end token <EOS> can be hidden. The guide said not to, but a model that never has to guess <EOS> can never decide where its story ends.

Idea 4 Looking both ways (bidirectional attention)

To fill a blank, it helps to read what comes after it.

Inside a Transformer, attention decides which other positions each position can look at. An AR model uses a causal mask: each position sees only what is to its left, because when writing left to right, the right side doesn't exist yet. A diffusion model fills blanks anywhere, so it uses bidirectional attention: every position sees the whole window. That's the only change to Project 1's model.

Who can see whom? Rows are the position being predicted; a filled cell means "can look at"

We wrote a test for this: change a word to the right of a blank, and the prediction for the blank must change. We then forced a causal mask on purpose, and the test failed, as it should.

Idea 5 The training loss

Only the blanks are marked; the visible words are just help.

For each hidden position the model gives a probability to all 8,193 tokens. The loss is the usual cross-entropy, −log(probability of the correct token), averaged over the hidden positions only:

loss = average over hidden positions of  −log p(correct token | the visible tokens)

We also watch a second number, the NELBO. It weights each example by 1/t, so examples with few blanks count more. The theory says that eNELBO is an upper bound on the model's perplexity: the true perplexity is at most this number. We can't compute the exact perplexity of a diffusion model, so this bound is how we compare it with AR models (Idea 8).

a fill-in-the-blanks exam where only the blanks are graded, but you can read the rest of the page to help you.

Idea 6 Writing by unmasking: three choices per step

How many blanks to fill, which ones, and with what.

At each step the model guesses every blank. Then three rules decide what we keep:

  • Schedule: how many blanks to fill in this step. Linear fills the same number each step. Cosine fills few at the start (when the model knows little) and many at the end. Confidence is linear, plus every blank the model is at least 90% sure of.
  • Order: which blanks. The ones the model is most sure of (confidence order), or random ones (Idea 7).
  • Sampling: what token goes in. Always the top guess (argmax), or a random pick from the top guesses (temperature, top-k, top-p), as in Project 1.

A filled token is never changed again. On the last step every remaining blank is filled.

How many of the 249 blanks each schedule fills per step (computed with our generation code's formula)

Idea 7 Which blanks first? The biggest surprise

Filling the "easy" blanks first makes the model repeat itself.

It sounds sensible to fill the blanks the model is most sure of first. But the model is most sure about common phrases like "play with the ball". It puts them in many places in the first steps, and the later steps then copy them. Filling random blanks instead spreads the decisions out and removes the repetition. The text is a bit less smooth, but not stuck in a loop.

Idea 8 Measuring quality fairly

Diffusion and AR models don't give the same kind of number, so we use three yardsticks.

  • Perplexity on the same test text. AR models give an exact perplexity. Diffusion models give only an upper bound, eNELBO (Idea 5). We compute both on the same 1,000 test windows. If the diffusion bound is far above the AR value, the AR model is better there.
  • Teacher perplexity of a generated story. We let the Project 1 teacher read each generated story and report its perplexity: how surprising the story is to a good reader. Lower means more fluent. It works for any model. (When the teacher scores its own stories the number is too low, so we use it only as a reference.)
  • Variety and loops. Low teacher perplexity can also come from repeating "He sees the ball" again and again. So we always report Distinct-2 (the share of different word pairs; higher means more variety) and the repetition flag (the share of stories that loop).

judging essays by how naturally they read, but also checking that nobody wrote the same sentence ten times.

Idea 10 A fair comparison: practice prompts and a rule fixed in advance

Choose settings on practice data, then test once.

A diffusion model has many settings: 3 schedules × 7 sampling rules × 4 step counts × 2 orders = 168. If we picked the best one on the test prompts, the test score would look better than it really is. So:

  • All 168 settings were tried on practice prompts (beginnings of validation stories).
  • A selection rule written down in advance picked the winner: the lowest teacher perplexity among settings with Distinct-2 ≥ 0.70 and at most 15% repetitive stories.
  • Only that winner was run on the 8 test prompts (the same ones as Project 1), 5 stories each.
Honest note: with confidence order, no setting of the small model passed the rule, because of the repetition (Idea 7). We then added random order to the search (and a backup rule), before any result of the big model existed, and wrote this change down. The backup rule was never needed.

13

Error analysis

120 stories, mixed and labelled without knowing the author

What and why

60 stories from the diffusion model (selected setting) and 60 from the AR model of the same size, from the same 8 test prompts, were shuffled and labelled blind. Categories from Project 1, plus one new one: local incoherence, a word that doesn't fit its neighbours (doubled words, stray punctuation, a word in an impossible place).

Who labelled: the labels were produced by an AI assistant (Claude), not by a human annotator, and have not been checked by a person. Blinding is imperfect: broken diffusion text is easy to recognise.

Result

  • Local incoherence is the diffusion signature: 97% vs 7%. Tokens filled in the same step can't see each other's choice, so neighbours clash.
  • Grammar errors (100% vs 27%) and character mix-ups (92% vs 62%) follow from the same cause.
  • Contradictions are near 100% for both with this strict rule, so that category doesn't tell them apart.
  • Loops are rare for both (3% vs 7%) thanks to random order.

Final report

The answer to the one question

Answer

  1. Quality: at equal size and data the diffusion model is clearly worse than the AR model, in perplexity (≤ 11.7 vs 5.24) and in the stories it writes. Even the AR model 2.6× smaller wins.
  2. Speed: 12.9× faster at 4 steps, but those stories are unreadable. At 32 steps (its best stories) it is about as fast as the smaller AR model. A key-value cache would make the AR models faster still.
  3. Reveal order is the key setting: confidence order loops, random order doesn't.
  4. Size helps a lot and both models were still learning, so the gap may shrink with bigger models and longer training. At this tiny size and budget it does not close.
Limitations: CPU only; one training seed per model; one dataset; training times slowed by other programs; the selection rule was changed once (written down before the base results); error labels by an AI assistant, not human-verified.

How we decided we were done

The question has an answer backed by three kinds of evidence (likelihood, stories, speed) and an error analysis that explains it. Project 3 is finished.

Lessons learned (useful for any ML project)

  • Match the comparison. Same size, same data, same tokens, same test: then a difference means something.
  • Test the one thing you changed, and break it on purpose. The right-side attention test proved itself by failing with a causal mask.
  • Check the easy task before the hard one. The fill-in test showed the model works before free writing showed it struggles.
  • A single number can lie. The lowest perplexity in the grid (7.0) came from stories that loop.
  • Tune on practice data, test once, and write down any rule change and when you made it.
  • "Faster" needs a quality next to it. 12.9× faster means little if the stories are unreadable.
  • Read the outputs. The doubled words explain the numbers better than any table.

Glossary

From Projects 1–2
Language model
a program that gives a probability to every possible token at a position.
Token / tokenizer
a piece of text with a number / the tool that cuts text into tokens (8,192 + [MASK] here).
Parameter
one number inside the model that training adjusts (5.3M and 13.9M here).
Transformer, layer, width
the model design; layers are stacked blocks; width is the length of each token's number list (256 or 384).
Cross-entropy (loss)
−log(probability of the correct token). Lower is better.
Perplexity
eloss: roughly how many tokens the model is choosing between. Lower is better.
Train / validation / test
data to learn from / to check progress and choose settings / for the final numbers only.
Top-k, top-p, temperature, argmax
ways to pick a token from the probabilities: from the k best / from the smallest set covering p / after flattening or sharpening / always the best.
Distinct-2
share of different word pairs in a story: a measure of variety.
Teacher / AR teacher
Project 1's 13.9M left-to-right model; here a comparison model and the reader that scores stories.
New in Project 3
Autoregressive (AR)
writing left to right, one token per model call.
Diffusion language model
a model that writes by filling blanks in parallel over a few steps.
Masked (absorbing) diffusion
the kind used here: the "noise" is replacing tokens with [MASK].
[MASK]
the blank token (number 8192).
Masking level t
the chance that each token is hidden in a training example (0.001 to 1).
Denoiser
the model that guesses the hidden tokens.
Causal / bidirectional attention
each position sees only its left side / sees the whole window.
NELBO, perplexity bound
a training quantity whose exponential is an upper limit on the true perplexity of a diffusion model.
Step (refinement step)
one round of guessing all blanks and filling some; one model call.
Schedule (linear, cosine, confidence)
how many blanks are filled at each step.
Reveal order (confidence, random)
which blanks are filled first.
Teacher perplexity
how surprising a generated story is to the AR teacher; lower means more fluent.
Repetition flag
marks a story that repeats the same phrases too often (Project 1's rule).
Local incoherence
a word that doesn't fit its neighbours: doubled words, stray punctuation, impossible slots.
Practice (dev) prompts
story beginnings from the validation set, used only to choose settings.
Key-value cache
a speed trick for AR models that reuses earlier work instead of recomputing it; not used here.
Ablation
an experiment that changes one setting to see how much it matters.
Blind labelling
judging outputs without knowing which model wrote them.
